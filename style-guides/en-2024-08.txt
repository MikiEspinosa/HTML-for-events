THE A TO Z OF IESE STYLE 
 
 
LATEST UPDATE: AUG ‘24 
 
We follow American English spelling, style and usage (for more information on American English spelling, see 
English in this guide), and use the AP Stylebook as our main guide for language-related issues. 
 
For questions on specific words, go to the Merriam-Webster Dictionary. For additional queries or comments, 
send an email to cmeans@iese.edu. The guide will be continuously updated. 
 
A 
 
abbreviations In running text, periods but no spaces between letters for most two-letter abbreviations, 
expressions of time and names of countries. Example: The U.S. election results came out at 10 p.m. Also U.S., 
U.N., U.K., B.A., B.C. 
 
For brochures and other marketing materials, along with social media, the periods can be omitted. Example: 
Join us for a webinar at 1:30 pm! 
 
Exception: No periods for academic degrees or for GI, ID or EU. Also, use all caps, but no periods, in longer 
abbreviations when the individual letters are pronounced: ABC, CIA, FBI. 
 
academic degrees Apostrophe and lower case in bachelor’s degree and master’s degree. No apostrophe and 
upper case when the full name of the degree is used. Example: Bachelor of Arts (BA) and Master of Business 
Administration (MBA). No periods needed. 
 
Lower case for doctoral degree or doctorate. No periods in PhD. For clarity’s sake, it’s sometimes preferable to 
use the phrase instead of an abbreviation. Example: Pablo Fernandez, who has a doctorate in financial 
management, was the principal researcher. Do not use both the courtesy title for an academic degree and the 
name of the degree in the same reference. Example: Dr. Robert Smith, a nuclear physicist, not Dr. Robert 
Smith, PhD in nuclear physics. 
 
Some other degrees and their abbreviations that frequently appear in IESE texts: Bachelor of Science (BSc), 
Bachelor of Arts (BA), MSc (Master of Science), Master of Arts (MA), Doctor of Science (DSc).

academic year IESE’s preferred style for writing academic years is 2018–19, not 2018–2019 or 2018/19. 
 
accents Respect accentuation in individuals’ names whenever possible. Examples: Francesc Macià, Antón 
Martín. 
 
acronyms Spell out the name, title or phrase on first reference, followed by the acronym in parentheses. 
Example: The International Monetary Fund (IMF) approved the loan. Acronyms are acceptable thereafter. 
Don’t use acronyms if there’s only one reference. 
 
adviser Not advisor. 
 
affect/effect The verb affect means to influence or produce a change. Effect as a verb means to bring about, 
but the word’s more common use is as a noun meaning the result. Examples: The changes in post-tenure 
review will affect all departments. The committee effected many changes in the review process. The dean’s 
office felt the effects of the changes. 
 
age Use figures. When expressed as an adjective, use hyphens. Example: The 28-year-old MBA student. But: 
The MBA student is 28 years old. Avoid referring to age unless it’s relevant.

Alumni Association Always capitalize when the words are used together as the name of IESE’s Alumni 
Association. Do not capitalize association when it stands alone. Examples: The IESE Alumni Association 
includes members from all over the world. The association’s executive body meets once a year. 
 
Do not capitalize the word alumni when it stands alone. Example: Dean Franz Heukamp met with alumni in 
Asia last month. 
 
alumnus, alumni, alumna, alumnae Use alumnus (alumni in the plural) to refer to a male graduate. Use alumna 
(alumnae in the plural) for a female graduate. Use alumni to refer to a group of men and women. The word 
alum is also acceptable in more informal contexts. 
 
Identify past and current students by using the abbreviation for the academic degree with the last two digits of 
the graduation year, in parenthesis. Example: John Smith (MBA-02). If a person received more than one 
degree from IESE, include both degrees and years and put a comma between them. Example: John Smith 
(MBA-89, PhD-94). 
 
a.m./p.m. Lower case and with periods in running text. Periods can be omitted in texts (such as invitations, 
landing pages, etc) when time is not part of a complete sentence. 
 
analyses Is the plural of analysis. 
 
app Short for application and acceptable on first reference. Lower case. 
 
associated schools The more than a dozen business schools around the world that IESE has helped to 
develop (for more information, see the IESE website).

books Titles of books go in italics. Example: Everyone should read The Ingenious Nobleman Sir Quixote of La

bulleted lists Follow these rules for creating bulleted lists: 
 For simple word or phrase lists, do not punctuate. End complete sentences with a period. 
 Capitalize the first word of a list item only if the item is a complete sentence. 
 Structure all items within a list similarly. If any item on a list is punctuated, punctuate every item.

capitalization Avoid using ALL CAPS in headlines or text. 
 
Avoid unnecessary capitals, but use for proper nouns and official names. Unofficial, informal, shortened or 
generic names are not capitalized. Exception: Do not capitalize committee in shortened versions of long 
committee names: The Senate Banking, Housing and Urban Affairs Committee, for example, became the 
Senate banking committee.  
Examples: 
 The Metropolitan Museum of Art is no longer free, but museum lovers still go there. 
 Office of Admissions, the admissions official interviewed candidates 
 The president of France is the head of state, while the prime minister is the head of government. 
 IESE Business School, the business school, the school 
 She taught a course called Basic Mathematics and Statistics to teach students the math basics. 
 
Do not capitalize the second reference to the committee using a shortened version. Example: The state Board 
of Health meets the third Wednesday of each month. The board’s agenda is available about a week before the 
meeting. 
 
Use lowercase for internal elements of an organization when they have names that are widely used generic 
terms. Examples: the board of directors of General Motors, the board of trustees of Columbia University, the 
history department of Harvard University, the sports department of the Daily Citizen-Leader.

Catalonia Rather than Catalunya or Cataluña.

6 
 
 
 
 
 
center Not centre. American English spellings are always preferable except in the case of official titles of 
institutions (i.e., the Centre for European Studies). 
 
century Lower case when used with a number, and centuries normally cited with numbers rather than words. 
Example: 21st century. Avoid using superscript letters. 
 
chair/chairman Use chair instead of chairman, chairwoman or chairperson whenever possible. Exception: 
Those groups that use chairman exclusively, including when referring to women. 
 
The term academic chair refers to a title held by a professor whose activities are usually sponsored by a 
specific entity. Example: He holds the Nissan Chair of Corporate Strategy and International Business. 
Refer to IESE’s research chairs by their complete names within texts. For an up-to-date list, see IESE’s 
website. 
 
co- (prefix) Use a hyphen when forming nouns, adjectives and verbs that:  
1) indicate occupation or status: co-author, co-host, co-chair, co-owner, co-defendant, co-partner, co-pilot, co-
sponsor, co-signer, co-worker. (Several are exceptions to Webster’s New World College Dictionary in the 
interests of consistency.) 
2) form part of a formal title before a name: co-President Alexa Manola, co-Executive Director Alfredo Hudson.  
Use no hyphen in other combinations: coeducation, cooperative, coordinate, coexist, coordination, coexistence, 
copay, cooperate, coworking. 
Cooperate, coordinate and related words are exceptions to the rule that a hyphen is used if a prefix ends in a 
vowel and the word that follows begins with the same vowel. 
 
cocktail reception Usually better to use another term, such as reception or networking event. Examples: A 
reception will take place at 8 p.m. OR Refreshments will be served at 8 p.m. 
 
Colombia/Columbia Colombia Is the South American country. But it’s the District of Columbia (DC), Columbia 
University in New York City, Columbia Pictures and the Canadian province British Columbia. 
 
colons (:) Capitalize the first word after a colon only if it is a proper noun or the start of a complete sentence. 
Examples: The mayor made a promise in her speech: All neighborhoods will have bicycle lanes by the end of 
the year. There were several obstacles to creating bicycle lanes: financing, space and cars. See headlines for 
the use of colons in headlines.

7 
 
 
 
 
 
commas (serial) No comma before and or other conjunctions in a series. Use commas only when the potential 
for confusion exists without them. Examples: She served on the committee to review scholarships, grants and 
financial aid. The featured topics were business ethics, leadership, accounting and control, and strategy. 
 
committee names Capitalize the full names of committees. Example: The MBA Admissions Committee will 
meet Thursday. 
 
common noun elements of names in plural uses Lowercase the common noun elements of names in plural 
uses: the Democratic and Republican parties, Main and State streets, lakes Erie and Ontario. Exception: plurals 
of formal titles with full names are capitalized: Deans Xavier Vives and Jordi Canals. 
 
company names Follow the company’s lead in matters of punctuation, capitalization and other matters. Use 
Co., Inc. or Ltd., as it appears on the company’s website. Use company, not co., when referring to the 
company informally. Follow the same rule for companies from non-English speaking countries. Examples: 
Schleifring und Apparatebau GmbH, Radiometer A/S). Companies themselves frequently drop these 
abbreviations, however. 
 
compare with/compare to Compare to means “to liken, to point out one or more similarities.” Example: 
Scientists sometimes compare the human brain to a computer. Compare with means “to examine to determine 
similarities and differences.” Example: America’s healthcare system is being compared with Canada’s. 
 
corporate social responsibility (CSR) Not capitalized in most cases, unless it forms part of an official title. 
 
counterparts The adjective in front of counterparts indicates how they are different. Example: Women 
executives upstage their male counterparts. NOT: Women upstage their male counterparts. Usage is correct in 
the first example because counterparts refers to executives and the differentiating feature is male vs. female. 
 
currency See dollars and euros. For all currencies, use the symbol before the figure, without a space. 
Example: $20,000. 
 
cyber Follow the rule for most prefixes – which is no space and no hyphen. Examples: cyberattack 
cybersecurity and cybercrime. Exception: Cyber Monday 
 
D 
 
Dash/n-dash Use a dash to signal an abrupt change of thought in a sentence or to set off a series of words that 
would otherwise take commas. Example: Alternative story forms – such as Q&As, timelines, short sidebars and

lists – help readers understand. Use spaces on both sides of the dash. Make sure you use an n-dash, which is 
longer than a hyphen but shorter than an m-dash. Some computers will insert these automatically, but they can 
also be formed by using two hyphens together (--) or as a special symbol. 
 
Also use the n-dash, rather than the shorter hyphen, to indicate range. Example: The course lasts 18–24 
months. 
 
database One word. 
 
dates Comma after the day when writing the month, day and year. Example: The MBA Open Day was held 
Oct. 16, 2002. No comma needed when writing the month and year only. Example: She graduated in May 
2000. When a month is written with a day, abbreviate the following months: Jan., Feb., Aug., Sept., Oct., Nov., 
Dec. Spell out all months that stand alone or are used with the year only. Do not use the th, rd when using the 
day and month – just the number. Examples: Graduation is May 16. Not: Graduation is May 16th. 
 
For date spans, closed n-dashes are usually preferred. Example: May 16–18, 2012 or May 16, 2012–Oct. 1, 
2013. 
 
dean Capitalize when the title appears directly before the name. Lower case when after. Examples: Dean 
Franz Heukamp visited Tokyo. Franz Heukamp, who is dean of IESE, visited Tokyo. On second reference, use 
only the last name in most cases, except in more formal contexts, when Dean Heukamp would be used. 
 
decision-maker/decision-making Two words, hyphenated. 
 
different (from) Different takes the preposition from, not than. Example: The new statistics class is different from 
the old one in three ways.  
 
north/south/east/west Lowercase compass directions: north, south, east, west, northeast, southwest, etc. 
Example: He drove north. The storm is moving east. Head southwest until you come to the exit. Uppercase 
words if describing a region: Southern, Midwest, East Coast, West Coast. Example: She has a Southern accent. 
His family lives on the West Coast. They are Northerners. 
 
 
dollars Always lowercase. Use numbers and the $ sign in all except casual references or amounts without a 
figure, placing the $ before the figure. Example: The book cost $4 plus tax. Billions of dollars are flowing 
overseas.

9 
 
 
 
 
 
 
For specified amounts, the quantity takes a singular verb. Example: He said $500,000 is what they want. For 
amounts of more than $1 million, use the $ and numbers with up to two decimal places. Do not link the numbers 
and the word with a hyphen. Examples: It is worth $4.35 million. It is worth exactly $4,351,242. He proposed a 
$300 billion budget. 
 
Always use commas (never periods, as in Spanish) in numbers with four or more digits. Examples: $1,000 and 
$50,000, NOT $1000 or $50.000. Always use periods (never commas, as in Spanish) for decimals. Examples: 
The interest rate was 6.7%. The sandwich cost $5.50. 
 
For amounts of more than $1 million, use words rather than many zeros. Examples: $2 billion, $3 trillion, It is a 
$3 trillion sector. 
 
do’s and don’ts With apostrophe. But: haves and have-nots. 
dot-com With hyphen. Not: dot.com or dotcom. 
Dr. Normally use when referring to a medical doctor, rather than someone who holds a PhD/doctorate. In 
running texts, use with a period; in marketing materials, the period can be omitted. 
 
 
E 
 
earth/Earth Lowercase in most uses. Examples: down to earth; earth mother. Capitalize the name of the 
planet. Example: Aliens traveled to Earth. 
 
e.g., i.e., etc. The abbreviation e.g. means “for example.” The abbreviation i.e. means “that is” or “in other 
words.” E.g., i.e. and etc. are usually preceded by a comma. Example: IESE offers many different programs, 
e.g. the MBA, the Program for Management Development and the Programa de Dirección General. 
 
email No hyphen. In other cases where the e is short for the word electronic, a hyphen is preferred. Examples: 
e-commerce, e-business, e-learning. 
 
emeritus This word is often added to formal titles to indicate that the person has retired. Place emeritus after 
the former title. Example: Carlos Cavallé, dean emeritus of IESE Business School, is director of the research 
center.

headlines Capitalization rules in headlines are often determined by the graphic line. When they aren’t, use 
upper case for the first word of a headline and lower case all other words (even if it starts with a proper name 
such as iPhone or eBay). Example: IESE ranked first in the world. Exception: The first word after a colon is 
always uppercase in headlines. Example: The IESE way: Making all punctuation the right punctuation. (See 
colons for the use of colons in other contexts.) 
 
Use numerals; do not spell out numbers except in casual uses or formal names: hundreds instead of 100s; Big 
Ten; one of the first. Spell out ordinals under 10: first, ninth, etc. But: 10th, 23rd, 104th. 
 
Use single quote marks, never double quote marks. 
 
hedge fund Two words, like venture fund and capital fund. 
high-tech Not hi-tech. 
honorary degrees All references to honorary degrees should specify that the degree is honorary. Do not use 
Dr. before the name of an individual whose doctorate is honorary. 
 
honorific titles Such as Your Honor or Sir are not normally used. Example: King Felipe. Not: His Majesty King 
Felipe. 
 
homepage One word. 
 
hyphens Don’t hyphenate adverbial phrases (generally, those ending in -ly). Example: The department chose a 
radically different approach. Hyphenate compounds used as adjectives before a noun. Examples: a far- 
reaching decision, a small-business owner, a much-needed vacation, a thought-provoking article, a university-
related program. Capitalize both parts of a hyphenated word (when the first word is capitalized). Uses hyphens 
for date ranges, such as Jan. 1-4. There should be no spaces surrounding a hyphen. See dash/n-dash for the 
uses of this type of punctuation.

IESE is an acronym for the Spanish Instituto de Estudios Superiores de la Empresa, but we rarely use its full 
name. IESE is the University of Navarra’s business school. 
 
include Use when what follows is only part of the total. Examples: The program includes sessions on finance 
and sustainability (if the program also has other sessions besides these two). But: The program comprises 
sessions on finance and sustainability (if the program only has these two sessions and no more). 
 
initials Use periods and no space to ensure that both initials will be set on the same line. Examples: T.J. Maxx, 
C.S. Lewis. 
 
intensive work day Use to describe the shortened work day in IESE in summer (“jornada intensive” in Spanish). 
 
internet and intranet Lower case. Always use internet on first reference; in informal writing, net or the net is 
acceptable thereafter. 
 
its Does not take an apostrophe when the meaning is belonging to. It’s with an apostrophe is always a 
contraction of it is. 
 
J 
 
job titles In general, only capitalize when using a person’s formal title directly before their name. Example: 
Executive Vice President John Roberts. But: John Roberts, executive vice president. Never capitalize informal 
titles or job descriptions. Example: The writer Mary Beard. 
K 
 
L 
 
last vs latest Last usually implies final, whereas latest is the most recent. Example: In his last book, the author 
wrote about his latest disappointments. 
 
lead, led The two words are pronounced the same but have different meanings. Examples: Miss Scarlet was 
found dead in the library, having been hit over the head with a lead pipe. Professor Plum took the lead and 
solved the crime. The clues led us to suspect Colonel Mustard. 

=== PAGE 14 ===
14 
 
 
 
 
 
less/fewer Less is used with uncountable nouns. Fewer is used with countable nouns. Example: Fewer 
customers drank less coffee today. 
 
like, as, such as Use like before a noun. Example: She’s like her mother. Where a clause follows, use as or as 
if. Examples: He’s behaving as if he owns the place. You don’t know him as I do. Such as precedes an 
example that represents a larger subject, whereas like indicates that two subjects are comparable. Examples: 
Steve has recordings of many great saxophonists such as Ben Webster and Lee Konitz. Steve wants to be a 
great jazz saxophonist like Ben Webster and Lee Konitz. 
 
long-term/short-term Hyphenate as an adjective. Example: Her long-term plan is to set up her own business, 
but in the short term she needs to gain experience. When two or more compound modifiers have a common 
base, the base is omitted in all but the last modifier, but the hyphens are retained. Example: He suffered long- 
and short-term memory loss. 
 
M 
 
MBA Normally the abbreviation is used, even on first reference. If for some reason it must be written out, the 
correct full name is a Master of Business Administration. 
 
measurements Abbreviations for units of measurement are often acceptable, even on first reference. No space 
needed between the number and the abbreviation. Example: He went on a 5km run. 
 
millions, billions, trillions Use figures with million or billion in all except casual or imprecise references. 
Examples: I’d like to make a billion dollars. But: The nation has 1 million citizens. I need $7 billion. 
 
Do not go beyond two decimal places, unless numeric precision forms part of the content. Examples: 7.51 
million people, $256 billion. Decimals are preferred over fractions when practical: 1.5 million. Not: 1 ½ million. 
 
Take care when differentiating million from billion, especially when translating from Spanish. Example: The 
number 17,500,000,000 is 17.5 billion. Not: 17,500 million. 
  
Note that a hyphen is not used to join the figures and the word million or billion, even in this type of phrase: 
The president submitted a $300 billion budget. 
 
1 thousand = 1,000 
1 million = 1,000,000

Mr., Mrs., Ms. Are not normally used. If they do appear, they take a period. 
Munich In English, not München

names In most cases, use the first name and only the first surname. Two surnames are used only when the 
person is consistently known by both (e.g. Gabriel Garcia Márquez) or sometimes with common Spanish 
surnames (Fernández, Rodríguez, Gómez, etc.) where there may be some confusion. On second reference, 
last name only, with no title. 
 
New Year Capitalize New Year's, New Year's Day and New Year's Eve. Example: The dean made his New 
Year's resolutions. Lowercase general references to the coming year. Example: What will the new year bring? 
 
nobility Capitalize king, queen, prince and princess when they are used directly before one or more names; 
use lower case when they stand alone. Example: King Felipe is the king of Spain. Longer forms of a 
sovereign’s title (e.g. His Majesty King Felipe) are not normally necessary. 
 
No. (rankings) When ranking items, use “No.” followed by the number. Example: He was my No. 1 choice. 
Kentucky was ranked No. 3. 
 
non- Hyphenate except when the word has a meaning of its own. Examples: nonchalant, nonsense, 
nondescript. 
 
numbers/numerals In most cases, use numerals for numbers 10 and above, but spell out one through nine 
when they are used by themselves. Use numerals with percentages. Examples: He has finished four of the five 
chapters of his dissertation. Alumni giving was up by 5% over last year. If there are many numbers in a 
paragraph, some of them greater than 10 and some of them less than, use numerals throughout for ease of 
reading. 
 
For four-digit numbers, use a comma, never a period. Example: 3,450.

quotation marks Always use “double” quotation marks, not ‘single’ ones, except for quotes within a quote. 
Avoid using quotation marks to denote irony, nicknames, unfamiliar words, foreign terms or to give intonational 
clues, which are not directly quoted speech. Periods and commas always go within quotation marks. 
Examples: “Education is important for many reasons,” he said. His most popular article was “The Guide to 
Competing in the New Millennium.” 
 
The dash, colon, semicolon, question mark and exclamation point go inside the quotation marks only when 
they apply to the quoted matter. Examples: He yelled, “Stop!” Can you believe he actually yelled “Stop”? 
 
If a person speaks continuously for more than one paragraph, place quotation marks at the beginning of every 
paragraph in the speech, but do not place quotation marks at the end of paragraphs, until the final paragraph in 
the speech. When partially quoting someone, do not put quotation marks around words the speaker could not 
have said. Example: If the individual said, “I am horrified at your sloppy usage of grammar.” Wrong: He said he 
“was horrified at their sloppy usage of grammar.” Right: He said he was horrified at their “sloppy usage of 
grammar.” It’s better either to use the full quote or else paraphrase and not use any quotation marks at all, 
especially in cases where the speaker is using ordinary words. Example: Wrong: The professor said students 
should “complete their cases by Friday.” Right: The professor said students should complete their cases by 
Friday.

 prepositions of four or more letters (above, after, down, inside, over, with, etc.

 Capitalize to in infinitives: “What I Want To Be When I Grow Up.”

 articles (a, an, the); prepositions of three or fewer letters (for, of, on, up, etc.)
